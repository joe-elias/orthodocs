Orthopterans are deceivingly difficult to identify by sound. They are easy to annotate, because often their songs carry across an entire recording when viewed in sound analysis software like RavenPro. At least in our recording for eastern Texas, there are often only 1-2 species occupying a given frequency niche (less noise and other taxa vocalizing at night). Species identification, however, is a different story. Unlike birds, many orthopterans seemingly share the same song. Between like-species the only identifiable information is typically buried in the frequency range, note morphology, chirp rate, or pulse rate of the species. I found it quite difficult to find this information in a single location. Rather, bioacoustics information for Orthoptera was spread across different online resources.

My hope is that this page will act as a resource for annotators on this project. The page is a simplified collection of my notes supported by other resources which are used to identify like-sounding Orthoptera species in eastern Texas.

Trilling Oecanthus | Neoscapteriscus borellii

Just a refresher that the annotation tables for this project are set up with a Annotation column which gets the primary ID, and a Notes column which gets the secondary ID (if applicable).

N. borelli (Southern mole cricket) and trilling Oecanthus (tree crickets) below 3 kHz should always be included as the alternative ID and vice versa. For the purposes of this project their song is too similar to reliably provide a single ID, and they have very little information available that separates the two species at the resolution that we work with.

Trilling Oecanthus is much more common than N. borelli in the project recordings and should be receiving the primary ID most of the time. There are many co-occurring species in genus Oecanthus, each with minor differences in their song. Regardless, here are some clues that N. borelli could be singing:

N. borelli is somewhat frog-like, and sounds like a “small mower” on the woodland floor or is described as very bass-heavy. Oecanthus is more chime-like and melodic, however O. exclamationis does sound quite bass-heavy to me, but not as much as N. borelli . Oecanthus will often chorus in a continuous trill at a standard frequency. Multiple N. borelli might call at once, but with distinct differences likely present on the spectrogram because of the way that they project their calls. The morphology of their burrows act as a bugle for their calls to project across the soundscape which may cause slightly overlapped signals within 0.1-0.2 kHz of a single frequency range.

N. borelli will not deviate from a carrier frequency of 2.5-2.7 kHz. The latter is not helpful since Oecanthus exclamationis occupies this frequency range as well. Likewise, the pulse rates differ but our recordings are not of high enough resolution to parse apart most pulse rates in trills (~50-70 p/ch). Supposedly N. borelli will only sing during the first hour or two after sunset.

Summary: It will be Oecanthus 99% of the time from my experience, but technically there could be a chance that it is N. borelli if the song is under 2.9-3 kHz. I don’t have good enough ears to parse the songs apart, and the pulse rate is too difficult to view at our resolution.

Trilling Oecanthus | Gryllus rubens 

Trilling Oecanthus and Gryllus can be reliably seperated by sound. The song of Gryllus rubens (Southeastern field cricket) is much drier than Oecanthus (tree crickets), which is more melodic and chime-like. The signal of Gryllus rubens is more broadband—appearing as little slivers (Fig 1). The Oecanthus signal is a single thin, often muffled continuous line of sound. The signal morphology might be masked by background noise, which is why the sound (dry versus melodic) should be used as well.

Oecanthus fultoni | Velarifictorus micado | Gryllus fultoni

Oh boy… this one gave me a headache. The SINA reference recording makes Velarifictorus micado (Japanese burrowing cricket) sound very similar to Oecanthus fultoni (Snowy tree cricket) but this is rarely the case. I think the recordings are just short enough to mask the characteristic tells of V. micado, and a fast chirping O. fultoni. Nowhere does it discuss these two species sounding similar. Instead, V. micado and Gryllus fultoni (Southern wood cricket) are discussed as like-sounding species. It wasn’t until I came across our own recordings that were so obviously V. micado that I started to piece together the reliable information for ID.

Due to the variability in O. fultoni ’s song (Snowy tree cricket, aka the temperature cricket), these three species can occur very close in the frequency niche space. I’ve found that between V. micado and O. fultoni the note morphology looks similar.

I’ve found that O. fultoni gives a consistent and slightly melodic chirp, rarely breaking it’s tempo. V. micado’s tempo is FAST. However, this is not enough for an ID, since technically O. fultoni can reach a similar ch/s rate under high temperatures. Characteristically the tempo of V. micado is regularly tripping over itself—starting and stopping continuously in one bout of song, which I haven’t found O. fultoni to do in our recordings or any reference recordings. Also, V. micado’s chirp is drier than O. fultoni, sounding closer to Gryllus than Oecanthus.

That being said, checking historical weather data can’t hurt. Fahrenheit temperature can be estimated by counting the chirps in 13 sec. and adding 40. for the O. fultoni. As far as I know, as an ectotherm V. micado is obviously effected by temperature but not to this extent. So, check out what O. fultoni SHOULD sound like under a given temperature on SINA, and cross reference it to a historical weather database for Nacogdoches, Texas. If the tempo and temperature is WAY off, it might indicate that the song belongs to V. micado. Obviously pair this information with other auxiliary information described in this post to make an accurate decision.

Finally, V. micado and Gryllus fultoni can sound very similar. They both have a dry chirp at similar frequencies that regularly trips over itself. V. micado is typically faster than G. fultoni, and when in doubt, a band filter will reveal that G. fultoni has a characteristic 2-3 ps/ch and V. micado will have 5-6 ps/ch.

Xeno-canto observations for all three species and Strang’s Nature Inquiry post on V. micado were helpful here.

Gryllus pennsylvanicus | Gryllus fultoni

The song of Gryllus fultoni (Southern wood cricket) is inconsistent and trips over itself, thereby making it sometimes confusing to separate with V. micado. However, Gryllus pennsylvanicus (Fall field cricket) is the quintessential cricket. It will have the characteristic dry tone of Gryllus, but I’ve found that the tempo is more even-paced than G. fultoni. Basically the same case that I made for O. fultoni and V. micado. For the purposes of this project, I’ve found many more G. fultoni in our recordings than G. pennsylvanicus. G. pennsylvanicus’s geographic range is just on the edge of our sampling region.

Allonemobius tinnulus | Anaxipha tinnulenta

This issue didn’t appear until I was 1,250 recordings into annotating training data. The range map on SINA for Allonemobius tinnulus (Tinkling ground cricket) shows a range that is not within our sampling region, but the Song of Insects database begs to differ. Upon listening and reading about these two species it became clear they could not be identified by sound alone…uh oh. Thankfully, the naturalist records of Dr. Carl Strang came in handy yet again—Carl is a naturalist in Illinois whom I’ve never met, but his records on singing insects is a huge help for many of our Texas orthopterans whose ranges extend across Illinois and Texas. I’ll probably reference Carl’s naturalist blog—Nature Inquiries—a couple times throughout this page.

See this blog post. The note morphology can separate the two species, but only for high quality signals. This will leave out some low-quality signals but will at least help annotators capture high-quality signals from these two species for our CNN model.

Cycloptilum | Cyrtoxipha columbiana

Cycloptilum (scaly crickets) and Cyrtoxipha columbiana (Columbian trig) are similar sounding taxa that occur in the same frequency range (~6.4-6.9 kHz). C. columbiana has a song that is more melodic or chime-like than Cycloptilum’s silvery and dry song. However, this is not enough to reliably tell them apart in my opinion. To be safe I look at the waveform to count the pulse rate per chirp because it’s an easy tell. The key feature to look for: C. Columbiana has ~5 pulses per chirp (ps/ch) and Cycloptilum reliably has 2 ps/ch.

In eastern Texas, we have Cycloptilum squamosum and Cycloptilum comprehendens. Both have 2 ps/ch and occupy the same frequency range. Unfortunately, there is very little information about these two species. I’ve found 1 reference recording for each (please reach out if you have more examples!). It appears that C. comprehendens might have more of a two-step tempo than C. squamosum, but I know nothing about the variability of these two species’ songs due to the limited data that is available. For now, I annotate these songs as Cycloptilum. Not as specific as I’d like to get but it’s accurate, based on the current information and still speaks to the functional diversity present at our field sites.

Eunemobius carolinus | Eunemobius melodius | Eunemobius confusus | Anurogryllus arboreus

These species were actually quite simple to separate once I developed an ear for Orthoptera songs. Eunemobius carolinus (Carolina ground cricket) is the most common. This species is geographically widespread and calls loudly throughout the day and night.

First let’s discuss E. carolinus and E. melodius (Melodius ground cricket). E. carolinus should be a lot more common than E. melodius. It is a common species already, and E. melodius’s geographic range is just on the edge of our field sites. That being said I’ve come across both species in our recordings.

The most important identifier: E. carolinus’s song is VERY dry. The song of E. melodius follows its namesake—very melodic and bell-like, similar to a high-frequency Oecanthus. I’ve also found E. carolinus’s signal to be fuzzier than E. melodius, although this is probably because there’s more E. carolinus around to chorus all at once, creating a lot of reverberations around the signal.

E. confusus (Confused ground cricket) and E. carolinus (Carolina ground cricket) both have a dry song. It’s sometimes tricky, because E. carolinus will pulsatee and sputter like E. confusus. However, E. confusus is true to its namesake. It sounds really confused—starting and stopping, sputtering - stopping for seconds on end before starting again; maybe a slight ticking at its start up. E. carolinus won’t do this—even if its song may pulsate and sputter, it still carries on without continuous stopping.

There might be some debate about whether Anaxipha exigua (read below) singing ar low tempertaures could be mistaken for E. melodius. In most cases their frequency ranges should not overlap. *more on this to come - see this blog post!

Last, E. carolinus and Anurogryllus arboreus (Common short-tailed cricket) can sound similar—both give a dry trill. Their frequency ranges should not overlap, but just in case they do an easy identifier is E. carolinus’s sputtering and pulsating trill (almost drone-like) versus A. arboreus’s consistent trill. Also, A. arboreus is reported to only sing right after sunset (0.5-1 hour).

Here is a brief overview of these four species in order from lower to higher suspected carrier frequency:

  • All sing between 5.6-6.7 kHz.
  • A. arboreus: Dry and continuous trill. Does not pulsate or sputter. Sings immediately after sunset.
  • E. carolinus: Frequent singer. Drone-like trill that pulsates and sputters continuously.
  • E. melodius: Not very common. Melodic and bell-like trill that can start and stop again. Can be similar to A. exigua but isn’t reported to have the “chuwee” beginning and shouldn’t reach the frequency range of A. exigua.
  • E. confusus: Continuous sputtering and stopping. Trilling and stop lengths are inconsistent.

Anaxipha exigua | Neonemobius variegatus | Neonemobius cubensis

Please see the Nature Inquiries post one and two about these species. It does a better job than I can at describing the subtle sound differences between the species.

Basic things to remember: All three species crescendo—their songs start lower than their peak frequency and slowly pitch up as they sing to make a horizontal asymptote. A. exigua (Say’s trig) is common and LOUD. Many sources report it as being quite obnoxious with a bell-like sound. N. variegatus (Variegated ground cricket) and N. cubensis (Cuban ground cricket) are quieter and have a silvery sound. The key to A. exigua is that it crescendos like the other two species but at the start listen closely for a “chuwee” sound, similar to a motor starting up.
